Binance's hot wallets sent $6.3B to an attacker in under four hours. $2.7B is frozen. Most of the rest sits untouched in the attacker's wallets

Billions in crypto left the world's biggest exchange in a single evening. We followed every coin, chain by chain, to see how it got out and where it went.

01 · What happenedWhat happened in the Binance hack

On Thursday night, Binance stopped letting its users take money out. The world's biggest crypto exchange said it was looking into a security problem. By then, most of the money was already gone.

Binance is where millions of people buy and sell crypto. Like any exchange, it keeps its customers' coins in wallets it controls, and it pays out withdrawals from a few of those wallets that stay online all the time.

That evening, in under four hours, those wallets sent coins worth about $6.3 billion to new addresses on nine blockchains. Ethereum, Bitcoin and TRON took the biggest hits. Binance says a hacker got in through an engineer's laptop and used Binance's own withdrawal system to send the money out.

Bitquery traced each transfer with its blockchain data. We matched every drain to a Binance wallet and followed the money to where it sits now. Tether, Circle, Arbitrum and four smaller token issuers locked $2.7 billion. Most of the rest sits in wallets that have never sent a transaction. Dollar values are at the prices of the moment the coins left, and holdings are as of 09:56 UTC on 4 November.

02 · The setupWhere an exchange keeps your coins

An exchange does not keep every coin online. Most sit in cold wallets, which are kept offline and are slow to reach on purpose. A smaller amount sits in hot wallets. These stay online, so the exchange's systems can sign a withdrawal from them in seconds, at any hour, without anyone touching a cold wallet.

Binance publishes the addresses of its wallets in a proof-of-reserves list, a public record that lets anyone check the exchange holds what its customers own. Each wallet drained that night carries the Binance proof-of-reserves label in Bitquery's address data.

A hot wallet moves only when the exchange's withdrawal system signs a transfer. That system is what the hacker reached.

03 · The nightNine chains in under four hours

The first coins left on the XRP Ledger. Five Binance accounts there sent 233.5 million XRP to an account that had not existed a minute earlier. Solana followed five minutes later, then Base and Optimism, two networks built on top of Ethereum.

A quarter of an hour later, the three biggest drains began within minutes of each other. Binance's TRON hot wallet sent out 613 million USDT. Its main Bitcoin hot wallet sent 18,270 BTC, all in one block. On Ethereum, the hacker set a trap inside Binance's busiest hot wallet and sprang it four minutes later.

Ethereum lost the most by far, $3.8 billion from three Binance wallets. Nothing else came close. Drains there kept coming for three and a half hours, and the last one landed after Tether's first freeze and after Binance had paused withdrawals.

The XRP Ledger account shares no address with the rest of the attack. But its transfers were built in the same unusual way as the others, with a higher fee than Binance's own payments that day. Binance's own estimate of the damage, around $6.3 billion, only adds up if those coins are counted.

04 · The break-inHow the hacker got in

Early on 30 October, Binance said its first look points to "a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop". That gave the hacker a way into one of Binance's internal services, where the hacker could "submit unauthorized withdrawal transactions". Binance said it found "no evidence of any wallet private key leak".

The chain fits that account. Every drain carries a valid signature from a Binance wallet. On 16 of the 17 wallets, Binance's system simply signed ordinary transfers to the hacker. Those transfers were built differently from Binance's normal ones, though. On Bitcoin they paid about twice the usual fee. On TRON and the XRP Ledger they used settings we did not see on any of Binance's own transfers that night. The signing was Binance's. The software asking for it was not.

On Ethereum the hacker went further. Since the Pectra upgrade last year, an Ethereum wallet can sign a note called an EIP-7702 authorization. It lets the wallet borrow a smart contract's code, a program that lives on the chain, and run it as its own. Four minutes before the main drain, the hacker's wallet sent in a batch of these notes for Binance 14, Binance's busiest Ethereum hot wallet. Each note only works when the wallet's transaction count hits one exact value, so the hacker brought 50, one for each count in a row. One of them matched.

From that moment the wallet ran the hacker's code. The hacker called it seven times over the next three and a half hours. The first call alone took about 167,000 ETH, 1.6 billion USDT and 62 other tokens in one transaction. Seconds later, a second Binance hot wallet signed nine transfers of its own.

Binance cancelled the note just after midnight UTC, almost four hours after it was set. More than an hour and a half of that came after withdrawals were paused.

The chain cannot show who asked Binance's system to sign. On 31 October the FBI said North Korea was behind the theft. It named the TraderTraitor group, which it also blamed for the Bybit hack, and listed 628 addresses it ties to the theft. North Korea denied it. ZachXBT and the security firm Blockaid had already pointed to North Korea, CoinDesk reported.

The hacker's Ethereum wallet got its first gas money, the coin that pays network fees, from Tornado Cash, a mixer that breaks the link between where coins come from and where they go. About 1 ETH came out of the mixer five and a half hours before the Ethereum drain, and smaller amounts the day before.

05 · The freezesThe race against the freezes

Stablecoins like USDT and USDC have an off switch. Their issuers, Tether and Circle, can freeze an address so that the tokens sitting in it cannot move until the issuer lifts the freeze. The hacker moved fast to stay ahead of that switch.

Tether's first freeze hit the main Ethereum receiver 37 minutes after the big drain, and by then 700 million USDT had already left it for two new wallets. From there the money ran down a chain of fresh wallets. At each step some of it was swapped, and the rest was passed on before the freeze arrived. Back at the start, later drains still sent another 204 million USDT into the frozen main wallet, where it is stuck too. Of that, 200 million USDT came from Binance itself. At 21:42 UTC, 78 minutes after the main drain, a wallet that Binance lists in its proof of reserves sent that money to Binance 14. Binance 14 still ran the hacker's code, and 72 seconds later the hacker moved the money on to the frozen main wallet.

Tether froze 11 of the hacker's wallets on Ethereum and TRON. Six were already empty when the freeze landed. One wallet passed on 130 million USDT less than a minute before its freeze. On Polygon, a smaller amount of USDT was frozen later that night.

Circle froze the 823 million USDC in the main Ethereum wallet three minutes after Tether's first freeze, before any of it moved. Smaller issuers acted too. World Liberty froze its WLFI token in the main receiver at 20:57, before anyone else, and later its USD1 stablecoin, as Zach Witkoff confirmed on X. Tether's gold token, Paxos, Ripple and First Digital followed. Together these six token freezes locked about $132 million.

Just after midnight, Arbitrum's Security Council moved the hacker's ARB and ETH on that network into a locked wallet. The council is a group that can act for the network in an emergency.

In all, 1.76 billion USDT is locked, 71% of what was taken. The other $729 million got out first. On Ethereum it went through UniswapX and 1inch Fusion, swap apps where market makers fill big orders, and through Uniswap's own pools. Some went into Aave, a lending app, as collateral for a loan paid out in ETH. On TRON it was swapped on SunSwap, TRON's main swap app. Our audit of Tether's freezes explains how the blacklist works.

06 · The loan60 million USDT that Tether's freeze does not reach

One of the hacker's wallets put 60 million USDT into Aave a few minutes before Tether froze that wallet. The wallet borrowed about $45 million in ETH against the USDT. Less than a minute before the freeze, the wallet sent the ETH and its other USDT on to the next wallet.

Tether's freeze stops that wallet from sending USDT. It does not reach the USDT in Aave, because Tether blocks wallet by wallet and that USDT was paid into Aave's pool. The pool lends out every lender's USDT, and the hacker holds Aave's receipt for its share. To block the hacker's share, Tether would have to block the whole pool and trap every other lender's money too.

When we read the loan at 09:57 UTC on 4 November, it was still open. Aave lets a loan stay open as long as the deposit covers the debt with room to spare. Here the deposit also earns more interest than the loan costs. If ETH rises about 2% from its price then, other traders can repay part of the debt and take USDT from the deposit at a discount. To take out more than a small part of the USDT, the hacker would first have to repay the loan.

About three quarters of an hour after the last loan, Aave's guardian, a group of signers allowed to act fast in an emergency, froze lending in 32 coins on Aave's main Ethereum market. Nobody could deposit those coins or borrow them there. LlamaRisk, a firm that advises Aave on risk, wrote on Aave's forum that 292 markets were frozen across Aave's networks. The main Ethereum market reopened the next afternoon. USDT there still cannot back a new loan.

We count the borrowed ETH in what the hacker holds, and we leave out the USDT in Aave.

07 · The swapHow stolen dollars became ETH

The hacker took about 200,000 ETH from Binance. Hours later, it held more than twice as much. The extra was bought with stolen stablecoins and the other tokens it took, and later with TRX. Many of those tokens went to a fresh wallet first and were then sold on Uniswap and other DEXes.

Speed cost money. On the stablecoins it sold through UniswapX and 1inch Fusion, the hacker paid about 16% over the market price for its ETH.

In all, the rushed swaps cost the hacker $119 million against market prices. Traders on the other side of those swaps kept $60 million after their costs. Our follow-up on who profited from the hacker's swaps tracks that money wallet by wallet.

The swaps also explain two figures that went round on X, $875 million in ETH and $234 million in TRX. Both counted coins the hacker had bought with stolen stablecoins, which is why they run well above what Binance lost in those coins.

Eight big Ethereum wallets that have never sent a transaction hold almost all the ETH. The Bitcoin taken from Binance sits in 19 addresses, most of them holding 1,000 BTC each, and has not moved.

Early on 2 November, the XRP moved for the first time. The account that took it sent all 233 million XRP, worth about $299 million, to seven new accounts. It sent small test payments first. None of the new accounts has sent anything since.

The Solana coins started to move a day earlier. On the morning of 1 November, the hacker's Solana wallet sent 485,277 SOL to one new wallet. Smaller lots went to more new wallets over the next two days. Four of those wallets swapped about 41,000 SOL for USDC and spread it across hundreds of fresh wallets that also carry money from elsewhere. We did not follow that money further.

Then THORChain reopened. It had paused swaps on most of its networks on 31 October, after a refund bug that had nothing to do with the hack. It restarted Bitcoin swaps at about 17:05 UTC on 3 November, and ETH and TRON swaps about three hours later. About 40 minutes after the Bitcoin restart, the wallet holding the 485,277 SOL began sending it back to the hacker's main Solana wallet. Between our 3 November read and 09:47 UTC on 4 November, the main wallet sent 779,097 SOL, about $72 million, to 91 new wallets. Almost all of them passed it on within hours. Of the SOL taken, 3% still sits in the hacker's Solana wallet and in the wallets it paid directly.

Part of that SOL is being turned into Bitcoin. Wallets one step further on swapped SOL for USDC and sent the USDC to Ethereum through CCTP, Circle's own bridge. From the evening of 3 November to the next morning, at least 10.8 million USDC arrived this way in new Ethereum wallets. The wallets swapped the USDC for ETH within minutes and sent the ETH to Ethereum addresses that THORChain had paid for the hacker's TRX on 30 and 31 October. Those addresses paid the ETH back into THORChain, with notes asking for Bitcoin. The hacker did the same with AVAX, after sending USDC to Avalanche over the same bridge. By our read, THORChain had paid at least 497 BTC, about $39 million, to nine Bitcoin addresses named in the hacker's swaps. Six of those addresses pass coins to one another in chained transactions, so one group controls them. They have passed 366 BTC on to 62 new addresses that have not spent it, and we count that Bitcoin as the hacker's.

Our 31 October update missed two Ethereum wallets. One has collected ETH from the token-sale wallets since the night of the hack and has never sent a transaction. It now holds 9,788 ETH. The other got its ETH through Relay. Together they held 6,042 ETH at that read, so our ETH count then was about 1% too low.

The hacker spent the early hours of 30 October pulling its ETH together. Soon after five in the morning, UTC, the main receiving wallet sent 17,260 ETH to a new wallet. Minutes later, four wallets the hacker had used to sell stolen tokens such as LINK and USDe sent 14,540 ETH into one of the stash wallets. About an hour after that, the second receiving wallet sent 7,647 ETH into another. All of that ETH stayed in wallets we track.

Later that morning the biggest TRON wallet moved. Within five minutes, it sent out all 638 million TRX it held, in five lots. Three new wallets got 398 million. The other 240 million went through two more new wallets to the wallet that pays out TRX in small lots. On 31 October all three new wallets sent their TRX to the payout wallet too, the last one that evening.

From the early hours of 30 October to 05:33 UTC on 1 November, the payout wallet sent 685 million TRX to 220 addresses and emptied. The parked wallets and the payout wallet now hold almost no TRX. At least six of those addresses sent TRX straight into a swap router. On 30 October we followed that route through SunSwap to two wallets we could not then name. They belong to a cash desk, a service that pays out USDT for crypto. The same desk took stolen money in the Ledger and CryptoBilis hack. Many other people use it too, so we do not count its payouts as the hacker's.

Another part of the TRX went through THORChain, a service that swaps coins between blockchains. On 30 and 31 October, 98 new TRON wallets funded by the hacker swapped 124 million TRX, about $40 million, through THORChain. Each payment carries a note that tells THORChain what to send back and where. The notes name 102 Ethereum addresses and 13 Bitcoin addresses. THORChain paid the Ethereum addresses 16,553 ETH and the Bitcoin addresses about 49 BTC. The Ethereum addresses also took about 4,000 ETH from other senders that we have not traced. They passed almost all the ETH on to 333 new wallets. None of those wallets has sent a transaction, and together they hold 19,985 ETH. We count that ETH as the hacker's. Most of the Bitcoin has moved on, and 8.2 BTC still sits in one of those addresses.

Until this update, we had read the notes on only one of those TRON wallets, and we counted 3,600 ETH from THORChain. The other payees and the new wallets they paid hold another 16,410 ETH, about $38 million, which is now in our total.

From midday on 31 October the hacker also used Relay, a bridge that pays out on another blockchain. The hacker's last TRX payment to THORChain that day was at 12:27 UTC. About half an hour later, THORChain stopped reading the TRON chain, and it did not read it again until 3 November. One of the payout addresses put 145 million TRX into a TRON contract that passes coins through, and took it all back out to 18 new wallets. Those wallets swapped most of the TRX for USDT on SunSwap. By 20:34 UTC, twelve of them had sent Relay about $34 million in USDT and TRX. Relay refunded some of those deposits. Relay's own records show its payouts landing on Ethereum, in 13 new wallets. Those wallets swapped their USDT for ETH, mostly on CoW Swap, a trading app, and moved most of the ETH on to 24 more new wallets. When we last read the chains, those 24 wallets held 13,853 ETH and had not sent a transaction. We count all of that ETH as the hacker's.

The payout wallet's other TRX was split into smaller and smaller amounts across hundreds of new addresses. Many of the THORChain senders above sit further down that trail. Some TRX went through swap apps on TRON, and at least 1.9 million USDT went into JustLend, a lending app on TRON. At least 5.3 million TRX went straight into 723 deposit addresses at HitBTC, an exchange. Other TRX and USDT went to services that swap coins between blockchains. We did not follow those amounts further, and they are not in our total.

The main receiving wallet has kept selling the smaller tokens it took. On 30 and 31 October it sold DAR, CTSI and PROM into Uniswap pools that held almost no ETH, and got about $490 for all three. The 908,793 PROM in that sale were worth about $4.6 million at that day's trading price. In the same block as each sale, trading bots bought the tokens out of the pools and sold them on. Larger sales went through new wallets. One sold 1.69 billion SOPH for 932 ETH, and others sold ZAMA, RE, BLUR and ROBO. On the morning of 31 October, those wallets sent 4,003 ETH into one of the stash wallets. That ETH includes 1,187 ETH from the VIRTUAL tokens the hacker took on Base. A new wallet swapped the VIRTUAL for ETH on Base and sent the ETH to Ethereum through Stargate, another bridge.

We found no transfer to a mixer. Some TRX reached the cash desk, HitBTC and other swap services. THORChain, Relay, Stargate and Circle's bridge all move value from one blockchain to another. On each of those routes, the coins we found either sit in new wallets that we count as the hacker's or have moved on through more new wallets. Anyone can watch these wallets with the Bitquery MCP. The addresses are listed at the end.

09 · The scaleThe biggest crypto hack on record

CoinDesk called it the largest crypto theft on record, and the numbers back that up. The previous record, the Bybit hack of February 2025, took about $1.5 billion. This one took about four times as much. Even now, what the hacker still holds is about twice what Bybit lost.

It is not the only exchange hack this autumn. In September, about $387 million left Bitget on nine blockchains, and our Bitget hack trace followed it out. In October, CoinDCX lost $16 million from wallets it was still using, as our CoinDCX hack report showed. Two smaller drains around the same time, at Polymarket and Predict.fun, also used valid signatures to move the money.

10 · What to doIf you use Binance

Binance says user funds are safe. It says it will cover all losses from its SAFU fund, an emergency pot it keeps for hacks, and from its own money. Two days after the hack, its chief executive said Binance had already replaced 90% of the stolen assets and had shared an updated proof of reserves. Deposits kept working. Withdrawals reopened in stages three days after the hack.

  • Rely only on Binance's official channels for updates. Scams follow big hacks.
  • Do not sign anything or send coins to anyone who contacts you about the hack.
  • Check the address you withdraw to before you confirm.

11 · The methodHow we traced the Binance hack

We started from every wallet that carries the Binance proof-of-reserves label in our address data. We looked for large transfers to new addresses that night, then followed each one hop by hop. We timed each freeze against the moment the money moved on, and read the final balances straight from each chain at 09:56 UTC on 4 November. Dollar values use the price at the time of each transfer. Holdings, and the dollar values for the THORChain, Relay and later Solana moves, use prices from that morning.

The full histories are also sold as files on the Bitquery Data Store, as the Ethereum, Bitcoin, TRON, Solana and XRP Ledger datasets.

Limits on these findings

Transfers were read in full up to about 02:10 UTC on 30 October on Ethereum and about 02:40 on the other chains. Later moves were followed on the Relay route to 20:34 UTC on 31 October, on TRON to 05:33 UTC on 1 November, and on Solana to 09:47 UTC on 4 November. Holdings were read from each chain at 09:56 UTC on 4 November, the newly counted Solana and Bitcoin wallets up to 11:28 UTC, and the hacker's Aave loan at 09:57 UTC on 4 November. Ethereum transfer data ran to 09:34 UTC at the first read. At 09:42 UTC, the THORChain payee 0x2a82 sent 407 ETH to a new wallet, which swapped ETH for DAI and passed it to another new wallet. The 1.3 million DAI in that wallet at our read is not in our total. Anything later is not covered.

TRX paid out by the attacker's TRON payout wallet was followed to the 220 addresses it paid, then through THORChain and, for one address, through a TRON contract and Relay to Ethereum. The rest, 69% of that TRX, was split into smaller amounts, some of it through swap apps. It was not followed further and is not counted in the holdings. THORChain swap notes were read on all 98 TRON wallets our address labels rate as the hacker's; eight more wallets rated only as tainted are left out. We count ETH in the wallets the payees named in those notes paid directly, if those wallets have never sent a transaction. That ETH includes about 4,000 ETH the payees took from senders we have not traced.

One of those addresses passed 145 million TRX through a TRON contract to 18 new wallets. We read Relay's public records for the deposits of twelve of them. Relay deposits from other addresses are not counted. Two other wallets took ETH from the wallets Relay paid and also ETH we could not tie to the hacker. They passed all of it on, so that ETH is not counted.

On Solana we count the hacker's first wallet and the wallets it paid directly. SOL that moved further on is not counted. Some of it came out as Bitcoin through Circle's bridge and THORChain. We count the THORChain Bitcoin held in new addresses fed by six linked payout addresses, directly or through relay addresses they share, that have not spent it. Bitcoin paid to three other addresses moved on and is not counted. We may not have found every payer, so the 497 BTC THORChain paid is a floor.

Until 4 November we counted only the ETH from THORChain notes on one TRON wallet, 3,600 ETH. The other notes add 16,410 ETH. Our 31 October update missed two Ethereum wallets that held 6,042 ETH at that read. Both are counted from our 2 November update on. The 31 October update also said 57 million TRX had gone through the TRON contract that passes coins through by that afternoon. The right figure was 53 million.

Dollar values use the price at the time of each transfer: ETH and major tokens every five minutes, other tokens once a day, BTC and XRP hourly. Three small Ethereum tokens had no price and count as zero.

The XRP Ledger and Solana drains share no address with the rest of the attack. They are tied to it by timing, by how the transfers were built and, for the XRP Ledger, by Binance's own total.

Freezes count only where we found the freeze on-chain: Tether, Circle, World Liberty, Paxos, Ripple, First Digital and the Arbitrum rescue.

The record shows that each drain was validly signed. It does not show who asked Binance's system to sign, or how.

Wallet owners come from address labels and on-chain behaviour, and may be incomplete.

FAQ

How much was stolen in the Binance hack?

About $6.3 billion, at the prices of the moment it left. The coins came from 17 Binance hot wallets on nine blockchains on the evening of 29 October, in under four hours. Binance's own estimate is about the same.

How did the Binance hack happen?

Binance says a hacker got into its systems through a compromised engineer's laptop and used its withdrawal service to send money out. No private key leaked, Binance says. On Ethereum, the hacker also used EIP-7702 authorizations, signed by Binance's system, to make Binance's busiest hot wallet run the hacker's code.

Is Binance safe after the hack?

Binance says user funds are safe and that it will cover all losses from its SAFU fund and its own money. Withdrawals were paused on the night of the hack and reopened in stages three days later. Deposits kept working. Binance has shared an updated proof of reserves and has promised a full report on what went wrong.

How much of the stolen crypto was frozen?

About $2.7 billion. Tether locked 1.76 billion USDT and Circle locked 823 million USDC. Tether's gold token and four smaller issuers froze about $132 million in other tokens, and Arbitrum's Security Council moved the hacker's ARB and ETH on its network into a locked wallet. Another 60 million USDT sits in Aave, where Tether's freeze does not reach it.

Where is the stolen money now?

At 09:56 UTC on 4 November, the hacker held about $3.1 billion, mostly BTC and ETH. The biggest wallets have never sent a transaction. The XRP moved to seven new accounts on 2 November and has not moved again. Since THORChain reopened on 3 November, the hacker has moved almost all of its SOL and is turning part of it into Bitcoin, through Circle's bridge and THORChain. The TRX went through THORChain, the Relay bridge and swap apps, and most of what we followed came out as ETH in new wallets. Some reached a cash desk, the HitBTC exchange and other swap services.

Is the Binance hack the biggest crypto hack ever?

By the figures reported so far, yes. At about $6.3 billion, it is about four times the size of the Bybit hack, the previous record at about $1.5 billion.
