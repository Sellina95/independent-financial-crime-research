Hours earlier the caller rehearsed the same delegation and sweep on three small wallets of its own. About 975M USDT and 823M USDC are still unmoved at 0x5227…2f8c at block 26301061 and can be frozen by their issuers. That is the most urgent fact in this report.

1. Mechanism

  • FACT: In tx 0xad99264d…298b (block 26300753) the caller sent an EIP-7702 (type-4) transaction to Binance 14. Its authorization list holds 50 tuples, each with a valid signature for Binance 14, delegating to 0x63e842b0…6fae, with consecutive authority nonces 0x1209dd7–0x1209e08 (18,906,583–18,906,632). eth_getCode(Binance 14, latest) returns 0xef0100 63e842b0…6fae, so the delegation was still in force at block 26301061.
  • FACT: An EIP-7702 authorization only takes effect when signed by the authority's own key. The caller cannot produce one for Binance 14 without Binance 14's signing capability.
  • INFERENCE: Fifty pre-signed nonces is a way to make sure one of them matches a busy hot wallet's nonce at inclusion time. Whoever signed had unrestricted, repeatable signing over arbitrary EIP-7702 messages from Binance 14's key, not just over ordinary transfers.
  • FACT: Binance 16 (no code at latest) signed and sent its own transfers to 0x5227 in block 26300772 (for example 0x51f263fa…39f, 28,005.6 ETH). A second, independent key was therefore used.
  • FACT: The 780M + 43M USDC moved from 0xEe7a…4055 (an ERC1967 proxy) inside the caller's 0x043ff9d2 calls on Binance 14. INFERENCE: the delegated code used an existing USDC allowance or role that 0xEe7a had granted to Binance 14. OPEN: who 0xEe7a is and what that relationship was.
  • Class (INFERENCE): compromise of signing authority over at least two Binance hot-wallet keys, through key theft, signer or HSM-policy compromise, or an insider. EIP-7702 delegation was the instrument that made bulk sweeping of Binance 14 possible. It is not a protocol flaw: no bug in 7702 or in the tokens is needed to explain the chain data. The chain cannot separate a compromise from an authorized-but-rogue operator action.
  • What would tell a hack from a routine treasury move:
    • Hack-like signals present (FACT): a delegation to a contract deployed the same day by an unlabelled outsider EOA; 0x5227 was a fresh address with no history before block 26300770; test transfers followed by splits within minutes; immediate stablecoin-to-ETH swaps (FACT, section 3), which a treasury move has no reason to do and which reduce issuer-freeze exposure; lookalike poisoning around the destinations; a rehearsal on throwaway wallets.
    • Routine-move signals absent: no flow back into known Binance wallets, no Binance-labelled destination, and no announcement seen (OPEN, not checked).
    • What would reverse the verdict: funds returning to Binance-labelled addresses, the 7702 delegation being cleared by Binance 14 itself, or a Binance statement describing a planned migration.

No step-by-step method is given here, on purpose.
